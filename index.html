01

What a payment really is

Forget cards and coins for a moment. Every payment, since the first ledger, is four simple things.

Cash handing it over is the proof

Card your signature plus the bank's promise

Transfer a written instruction

Crypto a digital signature anyone can check

02

Who is already building this

Four layers exist today. Each one does its job well, and each one stops before the hard part.

One layer passes the request. One records your permission. One moves the money. One checks identity.

None of them checks whether the thing you paid for actually arrived. That is not a mistake in any single layer. It is simply nobody's job.

The unused code
A web standard for "payment required" sat dormant for 28 years before anyone used it properly.
Who showed up
Forty organisations, including the big card networks, joined the effort in July 2026.

03

Where it breaks

Three failures, and none of them are bugs. They are things nobody promises to do.

Permission gets lost in the chain

Like a game of telephone. The third program only hears the second one. Your yes never reaches it.

Paid, but nothing arrived

You pay for a pizza. The shop marks it delivered. The receipt is real, the pizza is not, and the money is gone.

The assistant believes a note

Someone slides an instruction under the door. The assistant treats it as yours, signs correctly, and pays the stranger.

A payment going through is not the same as the job being done.

04

How big is this, really

Small, real, and growing in one specific place. The headline numbers are forecasts, so we label them as forecasts.

Two things are true at once. Payments for small pieces of data and computing are genuinely happening, every day.

And a large share of the early excitement was people paying themselves to make the graphs look good. Independent analysts put it near half.

The famous "three to five trillion dollars by 2030" is a projection from a consultancy, not a measurement. We treat it as an opinion.
